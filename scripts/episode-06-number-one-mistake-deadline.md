## SECTION 1: WHY DEADLINES EXIST — AND WHY THEY'RE SERIOUS (0:30–2:30)
*Show: graphic of a courtroom or statute document*

"First, let me explain why deadlines exist at all. Because I know what some of you are thinking — 'It's my money. Why should there be a deadline?'

Here's the answer. The county, the court, the state — they don't want to hold your money forever. They don't have unlimited storage space in their accounting systems. And frankly, governments have their own budgets to manage.

When you sell a property at auction and surplus funds are generated, those funds get deposited with the Clerk of Court. They're held in trust, set aside for the rightful claimant. But if nobody claims them within the specified window — that money doesn't just sit there indefinitely. Depending on the state, it either gets transferred to the state's unclaimed property fund, or in some cases, it's permanently forfeited.

Once funds go to the state's unclaimed property fund, recovering them becomes significantly more complicated — sometimes impossible. The specific rules depend on the state and the type of surplus.

This is not a bureaucratic technicality. This is the difference between you walking away with a check for $60,000 — or walking away with nothing.

And the tragic part? Most people who miss the deadline didn't even know they were on the clock."

## SECTION 2: STATE-BY-STATE DEADLINE COMPARISON (2:30–5:30)
*Show: state comparison table graphic — use a map or side-by-side list*

"Let me walk you through how this looks in different states. And I want you to pay close attention, because the variation is significant.

**FLORIDA**
Florida is where NLP Sales is based, so let me start here. In Florida, the rules depend on the type of surplus.

For **foreclosure surplus funds** held by the Clerk of Court — former owners generally have one year from the date the certificate of title is issued to file a claim. After that, junior lienholders may file. There are also provisions under Florida law where unclaimed funds eventually get transferred to state unclaimed property. The bottom line: if you had a home foreclosed in Florida, every month you wait increases your risk.

For **tax deed surplus funds**, Florida Statute 197 governs the process, and similar timelines apply, though the specifics can vary by county and circumstance.

**TEXAS**
In Texas, former homeowners have **two years** from the date of the foreclosure sale to claim their surplus. Two years sounds like a lot — until you realize that most people don't even find out about the surplus until months after the sale, and then the paperwork process takes more time on top of that.

**CALIFORNIA**
California gives former owners just **one year** from the date the surplus is deposited. California's real estate market is one of the most active in the country, which means a lot of surplus funds are generated — and a lot of them go unclaimed.

**NORTH CAROLINA**
Here's an interesting one. North Carolina doesn't have the same strict statutory cutoff for former owners under their upset bid process — but that doesn't mean you can wait forever. Competing claimants, junior lienholders, and the courts can complicate things significantly over time. And at some point, funds can still be transferred to state unclaimed property.

**GEORGIA**
In Georgia, excess funds from a foreclosure sale are typically held for a period and then turned over to the state's unclaimed property division if not claimed. The window can be as short as one year from the time the foreclosure is finalized.

**ILLINOIS, OHIO, NEW YORK, and others** — each has its own statute. Some are generous. Some are not.

The takeaway here is not to memorize these numbers. The takeaway is this: there is ALWAYS a deadline. You need to know where you stand in the timeline RIGHT NOW — not six months from now."

## SECTION 4: THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS — SIMPLY EXPLAINED (7:30–9:00)
*Show: simple text graphic explaining the statute of limitations concept*

"I want to take a moment to explain what 'statute of limitations' means in plain language, because I hear this term used incorrectly a lot.

A statute of limitations is a law that sets the maximum amount of time you have to take legal action on something. In the context of surplus funds, it's the deadline by which you must file your claim — or you may permanently lose your right to do so.

Think of it like a store return policy. You buy something, and you have 30 days to return it. After 30 days, the store says, 'Sorry, our policy doesn't allow it.' It doesn't matter that the product was defective. It doesn't matter that you have the receipt. The window closed.

Surplus fund statutes of limitations work the same way. The law doesn't care that you didn't know the money existed. The law doesn't care that you were grieving, or that you moved, or that nobody told you. The deadline is the deadline.

Now, there are limited circumstances where courts have allowed late claims — for example, if the claimant can prove they were never properly notified according to the required legal process. But those are exceptions, not the rule, and fighting for an exception requires legal resources and is never guaranteed.

The simplest, safest, most powerful thing you can do is act early."
