## SECTION 2: WHAT IS A SURPLUS FUND? (2:00–4:30)
*Show the process graphic: `images/surplus-funds-series-cards.png`*

"So what exactly is a surplus fund? Let me break it down simply.

**Step 1: You owe money on your property.**
Let's say you fell behind on property taxes. Or maybe a mortgage went unpaid. The county or the lender has the right to foreclose and sell the property at public auction.

**Step 2: The property sells at auction.**
Now here's the key. That house might have been worth $200,000. The tax debt might have only been $15,000. When the house sells at auction for $180,000 — the debt gets paid... but what happens to the remaining $165,000?

**Step 3: The surplus is created.**
That $165,000 doesn't just disappear. By law, it belongs to the former owner. But it gets deposited with the county clerk or the court — and it sits there, waiting to be claimed.

**Step 4: The clock starts ticking.**
Every state has a different deadline. In Texas, you have 2 years. In California, just 1 year. In Florida — depending on the county — you may have longer. But once that deadline passes? The money may be gone forever.

That's what a surplus fund is. Money that legally belongs to you — sitting with the government — waiting for you to come get it."

## SECTION 3: WHO IS ELIGIBLE? (4:30–6:30)
*Show: `images/probate-heir-claims.png`*

"Now the big question: who can claim these funds?

**Former property owners.** If the property was in your name when it was sold, you're the first in line. Even if you've moved. Even if you lost the property years ago.

**Heirs.** If the former owner has passed away, the heirs — the children, the spouse, the estate — may still be entitled to the money. This is where probate comes in, and we help families navigate that process.

**Other lienholders.** If there were second mortgages, HOA liens, or other liens on the property, those creditors may also have a claim. But after all lienholders are paid — whatever remains is yours.

One important thing: **losing the property does not mean you lost the money.** The house is gone, but the surplus fund is still there — with your name on it."
