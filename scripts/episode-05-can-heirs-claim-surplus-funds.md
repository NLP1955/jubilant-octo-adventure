## SECTION 2: HOW INHERITANCE WORKS FOR SURPLUS FUNDS (2:30–4:30)
*Show: graphic illustrating probate process timeline*

"When someone passes away and they had assets — property, bank accounts, money owed to them — those assets have to go somewhere. In most cases, they go through a legal process called **probate**.

Probate is the court-supervised process of distributing a deceased person's estate. The court validates the will if there is one, identifies the heirs, pays off any debts, and then distributes what's left to the rightful beneficiaries.

Now, how does this connect to surplus funds? If a person dies and there's a surplus fund waiting in their name, the heir needs to prove to the county or the court that they are the rightful successor to that estate. That's where the legal documentation comes in.

Here's what heirs typically need to claim surplus funds:

**1. Death Certificate** — Official proof that the former owner has passed.

**2. Letters of Administration or Letters Testamentary** — These are documents issued by the probate court that give someone — the estate administrator or executor — the legal authority to act on behalf of the estate. This is usually the most important document in the process.

**3. Proof of Heirship** — A will, a court order, or other documentation showing that you are the legal heir.

**4. Identification** — Government-issued ID for the person making the claim.

**5. Claim Forms** — The specific forms required by the county or court where the funds are being held.

Every county is a little different. Every state has different rules. That's why working with someone who knows the process — like our team at NLP Sales — can save you months of confusion and back-and-forth."

## SECTION 3: LADY BIRD DEEDS AND OTHER PLANNING TOOLS (4:30–6:30)
*Show: graphic illustrating Lady Bird Deed vs. standard deed transfer*

"Now I want to talk about something that not enough people know about — and if you have elderly parents or relatives who own property, this could be really valuable for your family.

It's called a **Lady Bird Deed**, and it's also known as an Enhanced Life Estate Deed.

A Lady Bird Deed is a legal tool used in Florida — and a few other states — that allows a property owner to transfer ownership of their home to a beneficiary automatically at death, without going through probate. The original owner keeps full control of the property during their lifetime — they can sell it, refinance it, change the deed — but when they pass, it goes directly to whoever they named.

Now, how does this connect to surplus funds?

If a property with a Lady Bird Deed was sold at foreclosure or tax auction — and there's a surplus — the beneficiary named on that deed may have a direct claim to those funds. They may not need to go through the full probate process because the transfer of rights was already established.

This is a planning strategy. It doesn't help you if the property is already gone. But for families thinking ahead — especially here in Florida where Lady Bird Deeds are recognized — it's worth knowing about.

If the property is already gone and there's already a surplus fund sitting there, we need to work with what we have. And what we usually have is a probate process.

If no probate has been opened yet, we may need to open one. And yes, that involves an attorney. But here at NLP Sales, we work with a licensed attorney network — and we structure things so that legal costs come out of the recovery, not out of your pocket upfront."

## SECTION 4: COMMON MISTAKES HEIRS MAKE (6:30–8:30)
*Show: red warning graphic or checklist*

"Let me share the most common mistakes I see heirs make when trying to claim surplus funds for a deceased family member. I'm not telling you this to scare you — I'm telling you so you can avoid these traps.

**Mistake #1: Waiting too long to act.**
Every state has a deadline. In Florida, surplus funds from a foreclosure sale have a one-year window from the date funds are deposited with the clerk — though the rules can vary by case type. In Texas, you have two years. In some states, the window is even shorter. By the time you learn about the surplus, find documents, and figure out what to do — the clock is already ticking. We've seen people lose their chance by just a few weeks.

**Mistake #2: Assuming you don't need probate.**
Even when heirs are obvious — an adult child of the deceased — without a probate order or letters of administration, many counties won't release the funds. Heirs sometimes spend months thinking they can do it without opening an estate, only to be told by the clerk's office they need court documentation.

**Mistake #3: Not accounting for competing claims.**
If the deceased person had a second mortgage, an HOA lien, unpaid medical bills with judgment liens, or other creditors — those creditors may have a claim on the surplus before the heirs do. This needs to be researched carefully.

**Mistake #4: Filling out the forms incorrectly.**
Claim forms for surplus funds are legal documents. An error — the wrong account number, a missing signature, an incorrect description — can delay or invalidate a claim. We've seen people file on their own, get rejected, and then lose time trying to fix it.

**Mistake #5: Trusting the wrong company.**
Not everyone in this industry is operating with integrity. Some companies charge enormous fees — 30%, 40%, even 50% of the recovery. Some ask for money upfront. We'll cover that in a later episode. But just know — if someone asks you to pay before you receive anything, walk away.
