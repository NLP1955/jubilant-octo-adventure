## SECTION 1: TWO ROADS TO THE SAME RESULT (0:30–2:30)
*Show graphic: `images/surplus-funds-series-cards.png` — the two-path diagram*

"Okay, so let me start here. Both types of surplus funds are created when a property is sold at a public auction for MORE than what was owed. That overage — that leftover money — belongs to the former owner by law.

But how you got to that auction? That's where the two paths split.

**Path One: The Tax Deed Sale**
This happens when a property owner stops paying their property taxes. In Florida, for example, if you don't pay your taxes, the county puts a tax lien on your property. After a period of time — usually two years — that lien can be sold to investors, and eventually, the property can be sold at a tax deed auction to recover the debt.

**Path Two: The Foreclosure Sale**
This happens when a homeowner stops making their mortgage payments. The lender — typically a bank — files a foreclosure lawsuit, wins a judgment, and then the property is sold at a public auction, often administered by the county clerk or a trustee.

Same outcome at the end of the road. Money left over. But the process that created it — and the rules around claiming it — are very different. And that matters a lot."

---

## SECTION 2: THE TAX DEED SALE — HOW IT WORKS (2:30–5:00)
*Show: county tax deed auction list or graphic illustrating the timeline*

"Let's dig into tax deed sales first.

When someone doesn't pay property taxes in Florida, here's what happens step by step.

The county issues a **tax certificate** — basically a lien — on the property. Investors can buy those certificates, earning interest while waiting for the owner to either pay up or lose the property.

If the taxes remain unpaid for two years or more, the certificate holder can apply for a **tax deed**. The county then schedules a public auction. The property goes to the highest bidder.

Now here's where the surplus comes in. Let's say the property had a tax debt of $8,000. But it's a nice home — maybe it's worth $250,000. At the auction, an investor bids $225,000 and wins. The $8,000 owed to the tax certificate holder gets paid. The auction costs get covered. And what's left — in this example, maybe $215,000 or more — is deposited with the Clerk of Court.

That, right there, is a **tax deed surplus**.

Now here's something important I want you to hear: in a tax deed sale, the former owner may have already lost the property before they even realized what was happening. Life gets hard. Bills pile up. People move. They stop opening mail. And by the time the auction happens, they have no idea there's money waiting for them.

That's where we come in.

One thing worth knowing about tax deed surpluses in Florida — the rules around who can claim them and in what order are set by Florida Statute 197. There can be other lienholders who have priority. But in many cases, once those claims are satisfied, there's still money left for the former owner."

## SECTION 3: THE FORECLOSURE SALE — HOW IT WORKS (5:00–7:30)
*Show: foreclosure auction notice image or courthouse steps graphic*

"Now let's talk about foreclosure surplus — which is actually the more common type that we work with at NLP Sales.

When a homeowner misses enough mortgage payments, the lender can file a lawsuit to foreclose. In Florida, we're a judicial foreclosure state — meaning it has to go through the court system. This process can take anywhere from several months to a couple of years.

At the end of the case, the court issues a **Final Judgment of Foreclosure**. That judgment sets the amount owed — the principal, interest, fees, and costs. Then the property is sold at a public auction, here in Florida often through an online platform called the Clerk's auction site.

Now — and this is the key moment — what happens if that property sells for MORE than the judgment amount?

Say the judgment was $120,000. The property sells at auction for $210,000. There's $90,000 left over. That $90,000 doesn't go to the bank. The bank got paid. That surplus belongs to the former homeowner.

*[Pause for emphasis]*

Let me say that again. The bank is made whole. And whatever's left — that's yours.

That money gets deposited with the Clerk of Court, and it sits there waiting for the former owner to file a claim. But there's a timeline. There are deadlines. There are forms. There are potential competing claims from second mortgage holders, HOAs, or other creditors.

This is exactly why people miss out. Not because they didn't deserve the money. Because the process is complicated, and nobody told them it existed."

## SECTION 4: KEY DIFFERENCES — WHICH TYPE PAYS MORE? (7:30–9:30)
*Show: comparison table graphic — Tax Deed vs. Foreclosure Surplus*

"Let me give you a quick side-by-side comparison so you can see the differences clearly.

**Tax Deed Surplus:**
- Triggered by: Unpaid property taxes
- Administered by: County Tax Collector / Clerk of Court
- How common: Less common than foreclosure surplus
- Average surplus amount: Can be significant, but often smaller because tax debts are lower
- Competing claimants: Other lienholders, mortgage companies, HOAs
- Governed by: Florida Statute 197 (in Florida)

**Foreclosure Surplus:**
- Triggered by: Unpaid mortgage
- Administered by: Clerk of Court
- How common: Very common — millions of foreclosures happen every year across the U.S.
- Average surplus amount: Often larger — we've seen cases with $50,000, $80,000, even more
- Competing claimants: Junior lienholders, second mortgages, HOAs
- Governed by: Florida Statute 45 (in Florida)

So which type pays more? In general, foreclosure surpluses tend to be larger — because the outstanding mortgage balance is often much lower than the current market value of the home. Especially in today's market, where home values have shot up, we're seeing bigger and bigger gaps between what was owed and what the home sold for at auction.

But both types are real money. Both types are legally yours. And both types require someone who knows the process to help you claim them correctly."

## SECTION 5: WHO CAN CLAIM EACH TYPE? (9:30–10:30)
*Show: `images/probate-heir-claims.png`*

"Now who can actually claim these funds?

For **tax deed surplus**: The former property owner is the primary claimant. If there's a mortgage or other lien on the property, those creditors may have a priority claim on part of the funds. But once their claims are satisfied, the rest goes to the owner.

For **foreclosure surplus**: The former homeowner comes first after the senior lienholder is paid. Then junior lienholders — second mortgages, home equity loans, HOA liens — may have claims. After all of that is resolved, any remaining balance goes to the former owner.

Here's the important part: in both cases, if the former owner has passed away, their heirs may be eligible to claim. We'll cover that in detail in our next episode.
